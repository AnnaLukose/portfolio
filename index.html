ABOUT/Anna

Curious to a fault, calmest under pressure, and happiest when a system finally does the thing it was refusing to do an hour ago.

The short version

I am a final-year Electrical and Computer Engineering student at the University of Cape Town, and I have spent the last three years quietly collecting the kind of problems that do not fit neatly into a lecture. Tuning a supercomputing cluster against twenty-three international teams. Teaching a neural network to find particles that are almost transparent, drifting in the dark, two hundred metres below the surface of the Southern Ocean. Explaining pointer arithmetic to two hundred second-years at nine in the morning.

What connects them is pressure. I work best when there is a clock, a constraint and a real consequence attached to getting it wrong — which is why I keep putting myself in rooms where that is true. The competition floor, the research group, the lab with the students waiting.

The rest of the time I am reading documentation nobody asked me to read, or building something from source because the packaged version hid the interesting part.

Give me the constraint first. The design follows from it.

How I work

01

Start from the constraint, not the feature list

On the cluster team the limit was a hard power budget, not a hardware wish list. Every architectural decision fell out of that number. I look for the equivalent number on every project before I write anything.

02

Build it from source at least once

Compiling large applications — including large language models — from source for the competition taught me more about how software actually assembles than any tutorial. It is slower, and it is the fastest way to understand what you are running.

03

Measure, then argue

Benchmarking discipline came from optimising for speed and efficiency under scrutiny. I would rather bring a profile than an opinion.

04

Teach it back

Tutoring a cohort of over two hundred and lecturing students from eighteen universities is the sharpest test of whether I actually understand something. If I cannot get it across in five minutes, I have not finished learning it.

Where I'm headed

I am looking for graduate work where high-performance computing meets applied machine learning — scientific computing, research engineering, or infrastructure that other engineers build on top of. Climate and ocean data is where I have found the most rewarding version of that so far, and I would happily stay near it.

South Africa's HPC community, and the CHPC in particular, gave me the opening. Passing that on — through teaching, competition mentoring, or just writing things down properly — is part of the job as far as I am concerned.

Computer vision · Research · 2026

Tracking marine snow to estimate ocean carbon flux

MARiS × CSIR SOCCO

Sinking organic particles carry carbon from the ocean surface to the deep. They are also faint, semi-transparent and constantly moving, which makes counting them by hand impossible at scale. This pipeline does it automatically.

  • Pre-processing and segmentation tuned for very low contrast targets
  • Frame-to-frame tracking across time-lapse sequences
  • A convolutional classifier trained and hyperparameter-tuned on domain imagery
  • Output feeds carbon flux estimation for climate research
